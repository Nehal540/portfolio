01The starting idea

Many people have a skill they keep meaning to learn. The obstacle may be the commitment involved in beginning: the cost of a course,a fixed schedule,uncertainty about a teacher,or the feeling that a casual interest requires a serious long-term decision.

Skill Bazaar explores a smaller first step. Someone interested in guitar,gardening,football,home cooking or baking could discover a nearby teacher,understand their approach and discuss a session before committing. At the same time,a person with practical knowledge could explore teaching as a flexible source of income.

The central entrepreneurship question is whether this exchange can create enough value for both sides to sustain a marketplace. The website is one expression of that idea; the business depends on trust,reliable delivery,repeat participation and a workable revenue model.

02Defining the opportunity

The idea canvas frames the problem as a lack of affordable,flexible and trusted peer-to-peer skill learning. It explores short,low-commitment sessions and a community that could continue beyond the first exchange.

The early audience is broad: college students,young professionals,homemakers,working parents and other curious adults. On the supply side are people with skills gained through practice and lived experience. The proposed starting point is local,allowing discovery and trust to develop within a city before expansion.

A useful narrowing already appears in the work: beginning with a limited set of skills and a defined geography. The live prototype makes this more concrete by focusing on Ahmedabad and five categories. This gives an early pilot a more manageable shape than trying to serve every skill everywhere.

03Separating a promising idea from evidence of demand

The validation framework asks five important questions: how often people might return,how painful the problem is,whether they would pay,how the idea improves on alternatives,and what could undermine the business.

The board contains proposed answers and assumptions,including willingness to pay and the value of peer learning. It does not provide a verified record of completed paid sessions,customer interviews or conversion results supporting those claims. These are hypotheses to test before scaling.

04Considering the alternatives

The competitor exploration compares barter-based exchanges,local social platforms,tutor marketplaces and recorded-course providers. The strategic contrast is useful: a free resource may leave a learner without personal guidance; a structured course may require more commitment than they want; a formal tutor may feel expensive or unsuitable for casual exploration.

Skill Bazaar’s proposed position combines local discovery,live interaction and flexible commitment. The comparison does not establish that all competing services lack these qualities. The comparisons informed positioning rather than establishing market superiority.

05Translating the idea into an experience

The prototype presents a journey in three stages: browse profiles,chat and align,then agree on a teacher-set price. This makes the person offering the skill central to the experience.

Discover a relevant skill. The interface offers five starting categories. A learner can begin with a recognisable interest rather than a large catalogue of courses. The category presentation pairs an approachable learning goal with nearby areas.

Evaluate the teacher. Featured cards display a skill,location,experience,specialities,example price,reviews and portfolio entry point. Together,these answer practical questions: Is the teacher nearby? Do they teach what I want? Does their approach suit a beginner? What does a session cost?

Talk before committing. “Start a Conversation” is a prominent action. Its intended value is to allow questions about goals,teaching style and fit before payment. The public page explains this clearly,although a complete working messaging service was not established during review.

Make location part of the decision. The prototype includes named Ahmedabad neighbourhoods and a map area. This supports the local proposition. The accompanying area list provides a second way to explore locality alongside the map.

Invite teaching as well as learning. Separate calls to register a skill and explore teachers acknowledge the two sides of the marketplace. Growth needs participation from both groups; a successful learner-facing page alone would not solve teacher recruitment or quality control.

06Exploring the business model

The working canvases explore commission revenue,premium teacher visibility,session bundles and possible onboarding fees. Commission values differ across sections: 12–15% appears in some entries and 18–22% in others. The current prototype,however,explicitly promotes zero platform commissions and no platform cut.

Several models were explored. The final economics still need alignment with the customer promise; the difference between the canvases and the prototype remains an unresolved business decision.

The broader lesson is that monetisation affects interaction design. A commission model needs a reason for people to book again through the platform. A zero-commission promise needs another credible way to cover teacher vetting,support,acquisition and operating costs. Optional visibility or paid services are possibilities already suggested by the board,but willingness to pay for them remains unverified.

07Thinking about scale

The research proposes beginning manually,including an early set of sessions arranged through existing communication and payment channels. This is a useful operating hypothesis because it allows the team to learn where coordination breaks down before building a large platform. The board proposes a first pilot of 50 sessions; this remains a plan to test the service.

The growth concept is city by city,using college groups,neighbourhood communities,local social channels and referrals. A scalable version would need more than additional listings: it would need repeatable teacher onboarding,expectations for session quality,cancellation handling,support and ways to preserve trust as the network grows.

The board also identifies a significant risk: learners and teachers may meet through the platform and continue independently. A useful response would be to test whether the platform can provide ongoing value through coordination,discovery or community. That is a strategic question for validation,not a problem already solved by the current UI.

08What the project currently achieves

Skill Bazaar turns a broad learning idea into a defined marketplace proposition with a city,starter categories,discovery experience and business assumptions. It demonstrates the connection between user value and commercial questions.

The prototype displays teacher totals,ratings,review counts and completed-session figures. These are treated as illustrative interface content because the project has not established operating data. They are not evidence of adoption or impact.

09What I would examine next

Next steps: Run a small local pilot,record the effort needed to recruit and support both sides,and follow the complete journey from enquiry to attendance and repeat interest. Use the findings to choose a revenue model,revise the prototype’s promise and identify which coordination tasks genuinely need software.

Reflection

What this project brings forward

The entrepreneurial challenge extends beyond discovery. A scalable service needs a reliable exchange,a reason to return and a revenue model that supports its promise to learners and teachers.
