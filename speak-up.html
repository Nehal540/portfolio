01The starting point

Having something valuable to say does not always make it easy to say it. A student may understand an assignment but avoid speaking during a critique. A young professional may prepare thoroughly for a meeting,then struggle to contribute when the conversation moves quickly or a senior colleague asks an unexpected question.

Speak Up explores the space between knowing what you want to communicate and feeling ready to communicate it. The project asks how a digital tool could offer repeated,manageable practice before people encounter those moments in daily life.

The initial research explored communication broadly: confidence,language,social expectations,nonverbal cues and the difficulties of digital conversations. The eventual design brief narrowed that territory to a more specific opportunity,helping students and young professionals build real-time communication confidence through safe practice.

02Understanding what makes speaking difficult

The research board combines secondary exploration with an interview guide and a summary of interview findings. The guide asks when people feel hesitant,how they respond when they cannot express themselves,what emotions accompany those moments,and what kind of support they find helpful. It also distinguishes internal difficulties,such as confidence,from external conditions,such as language and cultural expectations.

The recorded findings identify fear of sounding wrong,large groups and formal settings as recurring triggers. Their consequences include self-doubt,missed opportunities and anxiety about academic or professional progress. The needs recorded on the board include realistic practice,peer support,cultural sensitivity and constructive feedback.

These findings suggest that the intervention needs to address the experience of participation. Providing more tips alone would leave a gap between learning about communication and practising it with another person.

03Making the findings specific through personas and journeys

Two personas organise the research into recognisable contexts. Aanya is a design student who is comfortable with visual work but worries about phrasing,fluency and judgment during discussion. Rohan is a young professional whose prepared ideas become harder to express in meetings,particularly when hierarchy or spontaneity is involved.

Their goals overlap,but the circumstances differ. Aanya needs a way to contribute during critiques and group assignments. Rohan needs to communicate clearly during meetings,client conversations and professional exchanges. This distinction supports a range of scenarios instead of treating public speaking as the only communication challenge.

The journey maps extend beyond the speaking moment. They include anticipation,preparation,entering the conversation,being prompted to speak and reflecting afterward. Across those stages,the recurring difficulty is that preparation often stays silent or mental. Once the interaction ends,people may replay what went wrong without receiving useful feedback about how to improve.

Design synthesis: The opportunity is a repeatable cycle of preparation,spoken practice and reflection. Each stage should make the next attempt easier to begin.

05Learning from existing approaches

The comparative exploration includes speech-coaching tools,conversation platforms,teleprompters,course-based learning and other communication aids. The useful distinction is between tools that help people prepare content,tools that evaluate delivery,and spaces where people can actually practise an exchange.

Speak Up brings several of those activities into one proposed experience. A person can prepare,practise alone,practise with peers and reflect afterward. The intended value lies in this continuity across preparation,practice and reflection.

06Designing the practice experience

1. Start with the person’s goal. Onboarding asks what the person wants to work on,their preferred language and their comfort level. The goals include building confidence,improving tone and preparing for interviews. This creates a useful starting point for personalisation,although the screens alone do not establish a functioning recommendation system.

2. Give preparation a place. The Home screen offers a coach entry point,a warm-up,progress and a daily challenge. The warm-up combines breathing prompts and vocal drills. In the proposed journey,this is a transition into speaking rather than an immediate jump into a scored task.

3. Make practice situational. Self-simulation offers academic,workplace and social categories,with scenarios such as interviews,presentations,team meetings and networking. A practice view shows the current question,elapsed time and options to retry or finish. The situation gives the activity a purpose beyond simply speaking into a microphone.

4. Support practice with another person. The Arena includes hosting and joining a room,while the script bank offers prompts for interviews,debates,presentations,meetings and networking. The live-practice screens contain a timer,voice-level feedback,pause controls,chat and tips. These elements describe how a session could be supported; they are not evidence that live calling or automated analysis has been implemented.

5. Close with reflection. Session summaries combine example scores,a short coach tip and self-ratings for clarity,tone and empathy. The reflection is labelled as private in the UI. A progress report then provides continuity across sessions through practice activity and achievements.

07Visual language and interface refinement

Speak Up uses Mona Sans for the main interface,Nunito for navigation labels and Instrument Serif for the level numeral. Deep green highlights actions and progress,while cream backgrounds,off-white cards and beige borders create a calm setting for practice. The home screen uses 10 px card and button corners,soft shadows and muted taupe secondary text. This board draws directly from the app’s existing interface.

The strongest next refinement is to align typography,spacing,card treatments and naming across the journey. The feedback language also deserves attention. A phrase such as “Good start,you can do better” may feel evaluative to someone already concerned about judgment. A more specific,supportive alternative would be “You completed your practice. Try one short pause before your next key point.” This is a proposed copy improvement,not a change already made to the design.

Another design tension is the use of numerical confidence and eye-contact scores. They give visible feedback,but they can imply a level of measurement accuracy that the prototype has not established. These are example interface states. Their measurement method and usefulness need evaluation.

08What the project currently achieves

The work connects an identified communication difficulty with a coherent set of practice activities. It demonstrates research synthesis,scenario design and the construction of a multi-screen mobile experience. The screens make the intended practice loop understandable.

The current evidence does not establish that users became more confident,completed more conversations or improved their communication because of the app. The defensible outcome is the design and prototype,with an explicit plan to evaluate its usefulness.

09What I would examine next

Next steps: Ask representative students and young professionals to choose a scenario,complete a short practice flow and interpret the summary. Observe whether they understand the difference between solo practice and the Arena,whether they can identify one useful next action,and whether the feedback encourages another attempt. Compare a score-heavy summary with a reflection-led version. Longer-term evaluation would be needed to understand whether practice transfers into real conversations.

Reflection

What this project brings forward

The central design lesson is that confidence needs a practical next step. Connecting preparation,speaking and reflection gives the experience a clearer purpose than a collection of communication tips.
