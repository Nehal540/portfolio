01Beginning with the first response to discomfort

The project examines what happens when a construction worker first feels unwell. The first encounter with healthcare may begin with deciding to keep working,asking a supervisor for help,visiting a nearby chemist or waiting until the condition feels serious enough to leave the site.

For this audience,the decision is connected to work,income,familiarity and trust. A formal healthcare service may exist nearby while still being difficult to use within the realities of a working day. Understanding those early choices became the foundation for the team’s research and concepts.

02What the team recorded

The board separates what people said,what the team observed and what the team felt. This is useful because an interpretation of a situation should not be presented as if it were a participant’s own statement.

Recorded accounts include asking a contractor to arrange medicines,using nearby clinics,going home when a condition worsens,and returning to a hometown doctor for more familiar care. Other notes describe purchasing medicines from a chemist for smaller concerns and stopping a course after feeling better.

Site observations include heat exposure,physically demanding work,limited visible rest or medical support in some settings,and concerns about sanitation and support for women workers. The notes also record smartphone and social-media use among some workers encountered by the team.

The evidence is not uniform. One cluster reports no medical van near the site,while another mentions occasional visits and available first aid. The recorded contexts therefore show variation in available support. They do not support a universal statement that construction sites have no healthcare provision.

03Synthesising the barriers

The team’s synthesis identifies several connected barriers rather than a single lack of awareness.

Time away from work carries a cost. The board interprets delayed care partly through the possibility of lost wages. A service that asks people to travel,wait or return repeatedly may be hard to prioritise even when they recognise a problem.

Familiar help can be easier to approach. Chemists,contractors and hometown doctors already occupy a place in workers’ decisions. These relationships matter because a new service would enter an existing network of trust and dependence.

Support is not always visible within the routine. When care is outside the site or difficult to locate,seeking it requires another decision and another journey. The team therefore explored ways to make assistance present at familiar moments or places.

Needs differ within the workforce. Notes about women’s menstrual-health and sanitation needs expose a limitation of treating “construction workers” as one uniform audience. A general information interface would not,by itself,resolve missing physical facilities.

Digital familiarity creates a possible contact point. The observed use of social platforms suggests that familiar phone interactions could reach some workers. This is an opportunity to investigate,alongside options for people who cannot use a smartphone service.

04Looking at the service through several stakeholders

The board develops points of view for workers,contractors and healthcare providers. Workers need a manageable way to understand a concern and find an appropriate next step. Contractors and supervisors are already asked to help,but may lack a dependable route to formal services. Chemists and local clinics encounter workers when they seek assistance,creating a potential bridge to clearer guidance and follow-up.

This wider view changes the design problem. An interface can make information easier to navigate,but the service still needs a person or organisation able to respond. The project therefore considers both digital touchpoints and the relationships needed around them.

05Moving from findings to possibilities

The team generated “How might we” questions around access near work,timely information,familiar communication channels and links between contractors and formal healthcare. One prominent question asks how basic healthcare could be brought inside the construction site so workers do not have to leave work to seek help.

The concept exploration spans several kinds of intervention. These include payday health vans,a missed-call service,WhatsApp-based information,visual guidance,a health access card,on-site facilities and digital tools for discovering services or schemes. The board also contains more technology-intensive ideas,such as health pods and sensor-equipped safety gear.

This range captures divergent thinking; these concepts are alternatives,not a single final feature list. The board contains desirability,viability and feasibility labels,but the work does not establish a complete scored comparison or the precise decision that selected the final conversational prototype.

06The current solution direction

The prototype presents a mobile-style Health Assistant through a conversation interface. Its welcome message describes four intended areas of support: checking health symptoms,finding nearby doctors and clinics,registering for welfare schemes and keeping health records.

The initial screen offers Hindi,Gujarati,Tamil and English; several additional languages are marked as coming soon. The English flow continues by asking for a name,age,gender and area/city. A change-language control remains available.

This provides a concrete direction for the service: guiding a person through smaller conversational steps. Language selection appears early,and button choices can reduce the amount of typing at particular points. The interface is messaging-like; the published page is not proof of a deployed WhatsApp integration or an operational AI health service.

The service pathways remain prototype intentions rather than evaluated live services. The welcome message communicates the concept’s scope,but it does not prove that provider discovery,welfare registration or record storage connects to live services.

A design question emerges from the current onboarding: should a worker have to provide several personal details before seeing available help? A future iteration could examine whether some assistance can be accessed first,with information requested only when needed. This is a proposed refinement based on the walkthrough,not a change already made or a finding from participant testing.

08What the project currently achieves

The work makes the first steps toward healthcare visible as a design problem. It connects field observations with service questions,recognises several stakeholders and explores different ways to reduce effort at the point of seeking help. The conversational prototype gives one direction a tangible form.

The evidence does not establish reduced illness,improved treatment,successful welfare enrolment or saved wages. Those would require evaluation of the complete service,not only its screens. The demonstrated contribution is the team’s research,synthesis and conversational prototype.

09What I would examine next

Next steps: Test comprehension with workers from the intended language groups,including people with varying reading and phone experience. Use fictional scenarios to assess whether they can select a language,understand a prompt and identify a next step without assistance. Test a path that offers help before profile completion. Explore voice and non-smartphone alternatives where the research shows they are needed. Any health guidance or escalation flow would need qualified review and a real service arrangement before use beyond a student prototype.

Reflection

What this project brings forward

Seeking care is shaped by the conditions around a person. Time,trust,language and work routines need to be considered together; the interface is one part of that wider service.
