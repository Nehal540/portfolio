# Flames of the Forgotten.

Keep the fire alive. Keep the hidden workshop unseen.

*The virtual workshop. Environment design by Paras Chanana.*

01 / THE PROJECT

## A story you enter through physical effort.

Flames of the Forgotten is a multisensory installation centred on a hidden blacksmith workshop in Malabar. Visitors strike physical flint and steel to ignite a digital fire, then pump a pair of bellows to keep it alive.

The workshop responds through changing flames, sparks, smoke, and sound. Steady pumping protects the forge in the story; excessive pumping produces smoke that reveals its location. The visitor's rhythm becomes part of the narrative.

I worked on the Unity development and created selected 3D assets. My teammate, Paras Chanana, handled the environment design.

02 / DESIGN QUESTION

How can physical participation make a story feel immediate?

The project explores the labour and vulnerability of blacksmiths through a visitor's own actions. Rather than advancing the story with a button, visitors must strike, pump, and respond to what they see and hear.

Narrative setting: a concealed Malabar forge under colonial surveillance. The installation uses this premise for its story; it is not presented here as a verified reconstruction of a specific historical event.

03 / THE EXPERIENCE

Action → response → consequence

## Your hands move. The workshop answers.

A dim environment establishes the setting. Two familiar workshop actions give visitors a way to influence it, while the fire makes the consequences visible.

### Find the workshop.

The visitor enters the exhibition space and encounters the projected workshop alongside physical tools and a fire installation.

### Strike a spark.

Striking the flint and steel triggers sparks and ignition in Unity. A small physical action brings the scene to life.

### Work with the fire.

Pumping the bellows changes the fire. The visitor must maintain a rhythm while watching for the consequences of overworking it.

04 / MAKING THE EXPERIENCE

## Connecting objects to a responsive world.

The installation connects physical inputs to changes in a Unity scene. My contribution was the Unity development and selected assets; Paras developed the environment that frames the interaction.

The key relationship is between what the visitor does and what the fire communicates. Ignition establishes that connection. The bellows then turn it into an ongoing exchange.

### Impact becomes a spark.

The presentation identifies an HW-500 vibration sensor for the flint-and-steel interaction. A detected strike triggers ignition and sparks in the digital fire.

Physical input: impact. Digital response: sparks and ignition.

### Movement sustains the fire.

An HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor supports detection of the bellows movement. Pumping affects the fire and feeds into the narrative's distinction between steady effort and excessive pumping.

Physical input: bellows movement. Digital response: fire and smoke behaviour.

From sketch to installation. The early drawings explore how the display, fire, tools, and visitor fit together. The documented setup brings these elements into one space, with the virtual workshop projected behind the physical interaction area.

05 / SENSORY DESIGN

## Each sense supports the same story.

The sensory plan connects the workshop's atmosphere to the visitor's actions. Visual and audio changes communicate the fire's state; physical objects give the interaction a bodily rhythm.

### Visual · Read the fire

Flames, sparks, smoke, tools, and the dark environment communicate the setting and the consequences of pumping.

### Audio · Hear the response

Striking sounds, crackling fire, and sparks reinforce the relationship between a physical action and the virtual workshop.

### Touch · Participate through objects

Flint and steel and the bellows provide physical contact, movement, and effort as part of the experience.

### Scent and warmth · Extend the atmosphere

The presentation's sensory plan includes burnt-ash scent and a concealed heat source. These are described as planned sensory layers; their final implementation is not documented here.

06 / NARRATIVE CONSEQUENCE

## The same tool. Two different outcomes.

Pumping is more than a way to advance the scene. The story distinguishes careful rhythm from excessive force and speed. At the ending, the scene transitions into a narrative reveal.

### The forge survives.

The visitor keeps the fire alive without revealing the workspace. The ending connects patience with protecting the blacksmiths' hidden workshop.

### The smoke gives it away.

Thick smoke rises above the trees and alerts patrols in the narrative. The hidden forge is discovered and silenced.

07 / OUTCOME & NEXT QUESTIONS

## An installation. An experience to examine.

The presentation documents the Unity environment, physical setup, two sensor-based interactions, and the narrative endings. Together, these form an installation in which bodily effort influences the story.

The central design takeaway is the role of feedback: visitors need to understand how their actions affect the fire before the final consequence feels meaningful. This is a design interpretation of the prototype, not a measured testing result.

### What I would test next

Observe whether a first-time visitor understands how to ignite the fire and begin using the bellows without spoken instructions.

Observe whether visitors notice the changing fire and smoke early enough to adjust their rhythm before an ending is triggered.

Check reach, grip, effort, and alternatives for people who cannot comfortably perform the striking or pumping actions.

# SPEAK UP.

A little practice before a big conversation.

## Speak Up. Practise before the pressure.

Speak Up is a mobile app concept that helps students and young professionals practise everyday communication before they face a high-pressure situation. It brings together realistic scenarios, private rehearsal, feedback, and reflection.

The starting point was “lack of communication.” The research helped narrow that broad topic to a more specific challenge: people may know what they want to say but struggle to express it when they feel judged, rushed, or unsure of the situation.

This case study combines the supplied research board with the Speak Up product direction developed for this portfolio. ## Having an idea is different from saying it aloud.

A classroom critique, a conversation with a manager, or a video meeting can create pressure that preparation alone does not resolve. The research board describes hesitation, overthinking, fear of judgement, language insecurity, and uncertainty around nonverbal cues.

The opportunity was to design a place to practise communication with lower stakes, relevant situations, and a clear next step after each attempt.

### Design question

How might we help students and young professionals build real-time communication confidence through safe practice, so they can speak with greater clarity in academic and workplace settings?

## Understanding where communication becomes difficult.

The FigJam board brings together background research, a questionnaire, response charts, open-ended comments, a comparative app review, personas, empathy maps, journey maps, and a final design brief. Questionnaire topics cover difficult situations, emotional responses, coping behaviours, motivation, and preferred support.

The available survey screenshots show 13 responses to the situation question and 14 to the emotion and support questions. These are exploratory findings from a small sample; recruitment details and participant demographics are not documented in the supplied material.

| Finding in the board | Design implication |
| --- | --- |
| 7 of 13 selected presenting in front of a group as a difficult situation. | Include presentation rehearsal with a familiar context. |
| 6 of 13 selected speaking to authority figures. | Include meeting and feedback conversations, beyond public speaking. |
| 10 of 14 selected nervousness or anxiety; 7 of 14 selected confusion or self-doubt. | Make starting and retrying feel manageable. |
| 7 of 14 selected constructive peer feedback; 5 of 14 preferred learning on their own. | Provide a private route and make peer practice optional. |

The bar-chart questions allow multiple selections, so categories overlap. These numbers describe reported experiences, not improvements caused by the app.

*Research board: reported emotions before or during difficult communication moments; 14 responses.*

Open-ended responses describe missed opportunities, difficulty explaining ideas during juries, and a desire to speak more clearly. Other responses report little or no impact. This variation matters: support should respond to a person’s context rather than assume everyone has the same difficulty.

## From research themes to design priorities.

### 1. Preparation needs to include speaking.

The journey maps describe mental rehearsal, notes, and watching tips, followed by hesitation in the actual conversation. This suggests an opportunity for spoken practice rather than another library of advice.

### 2. Feedback needs to give people a next step.

The board points to a preference for structured and constructive support. A useful feedback screen should explain what to try next, rather than leave the person with a score they cannot act on.

### 3. Context changes the challenge.

Speaking to a class, disagreeing with a senior, and interpreting a video call are different situations. Scenario selection can make practice more relevant to the moment a user is preparing for.

### 4. Comfort should be a choice.

The responses support both individual learning and peer support. Private practice should stand on its own, with social practice available when someone wants it.

### Looking at existing approaches

The board reviews speech coaching, guided courses, rehearsal tools, language practice, and simulation-based approaches. I use that review to frame the concept around relevant practice and follow-through. It does not establish that competing products lack these features.

## Two contexts. A shared need to rehearse.

The board uses two personas to organise the design problem. They are design representations, not verified participant biographies or direct interview quotations.

### Aanya ,  speaking up in college

Aanya has ideas to contribute during critiques and group projects, but worries about phrasing, fluency, and how others will respond. Her priority is to rehearse a situation privately and receive useful feedback before trying it in a group.

### Rohan ,  being heard at work

Rohan wants to contribute clearly in meetings but hesitates around senior colleagues and unplanned questions. His priority is practising how to structure an answer, make a point, or disagree respectfully.

### The gap in their journeys

Both journeys move from preparation to hesitation, a pressured speaking moment, and reflection afterwards. The proposed app adds a practice-and-feedback loop before the real event, then gives reflection a practical next step.

## A short route from intention to practice.

The proposed core flow is: choose a goal → select a scenario → rehearse → review feedback → retry or reflect. Keeping that loop short gives the user a clear purpose for each screen.

| Area | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Home | Start or resume a relevant practice session. |
| Scenarios | Choose a classroom, meeting, interview, or everyday conversation. |
| Practice | Read the context, prepare, and attempt a response. |
| Feedback & reflection | Review the attempt and choose what to work on next. |
| Arena, optional | Explore peer practice after the private flow is established. |

This is the proposed information structure for the case study. The supplied board does not document a validated card sort or a usability-tested navigation structure.

## The proposed interface, step by step.

### 01 / Onboarding ,  choose what matters now

Ask what the user wants to practise and what language they are comfortable using. Keep the setup brief and allow preferences to change later. The purpose is to make the first session relevant without turning onboarding into an assessment.

### 02 / Home & scenarios ,  make the next action clear

Give the home screen one clear starting point: begin or continue a practice session. Scenario cards should explain the context and the skill being practised, so users can choose based on an upcoming situation.

### 03 / Practice ,  allow room to try again

A session introduces the situation and the user’s communication goal. The proposed controls allow preparation, an attempt, and a retry. If voice recording is used, the interface should explain permission, storage, and deletion before recording begins.

The design intention is to reduce the pressure of a first attempt. Whether it achieves that needs to be tested with users.

### 04 / Feedback & reflection ,  turn an attempt into a next step

Feedback should highlight something the user can act on, then offer a focused retry. Reflection can ask what felt difficult and what they want to try differently. Any automated speech analysis shown in final screens should be labelled as a concept unless its implementation has been verified.

### 05 / Peer practice ,  participation by choice

Arena is an optional extension for practising with others. It would need clear expectations for constructive feedback, reporting tools, and a way to leave a session. It should not prevent someone from using the private practice loop.

## What the prototype still needs to answer.

The supplied board supports a research-led design direction. It does not include completed usability-test results or evidence of improved communication confidence. The next step is to test the core flow before making outcome claims.

- Finding a relevant scenario: ask someone to prepare for a critique or meeting and observe whether they can choose a useful session without help.

- Understanding practice: check whether the prompt, recording state, retry controls, and privacy explanation are understood.

- Acting on feedback: ask the user what they would do differently after reading the feedback.

- Choosing the right level of exposure: check whether users can find private practice and understand that peer practice is optional.

Record task completion, points of hesitation, and participants’ explanations. Perceived comfort before and after a session can be explored, but longer-term confidence would need separate follow-up.

## Designing a place to begin.

The research reframed the project from a broad communication-skills tool into a more specific experience: a place to practise before a real conversation, understand an attempt, and try again.

The key design lesson is that access to advice is only one part of the problem. The way a person starts, receives feedback, and chooses their next step also shapes the experience. The proposed flow responds to those needs; its effectiveness remains to be tested.
